Q1 • 16 Marks
Let $V$ be the set of all positive real numbers ($\mathbb{R}^+$). Define vector addition as $x \oplus y = xy$ and scalar multiplication as $k \odot x = x^k$ ($k \in \mathbb{R}$). Determine whether $V$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$.
Part I: Verification of Abelian Group Under Addition $(V, \oplus)$
  1. Closure: For $x, y \in V$, $x > 0$ and $y > 0 \implies x \oplus y = xy > 0 \in V$.
    💡 Why this step?
    We must verify that multiplying two positive real numbers never gives zero or a negative number. Since $(+) imes (+) = (+)$, closure holds.
  2. Commutative: $x \oplus y = xy = yx = y \oplus x$. (Real multiplication is commutative).
  3. Associative: $(x \oplus y) \oplus z = (xy)z = x(yz) = x \oplus (y \oplus z)$.
  4. Identity Element ($e$): We require $x \oplus e = x \implies x \cdot e = x \implies \mathbf{e = 1}$.
    💡 Why is the identity 1 instead of 0?
    Under normal addition, $x + 0 = x$. But here "addition" is defined as multiplication ($xy$). The number that leaves $x$ unchanged under multiplication is $1$. Since $1 > 0$, $1 \in V$. Thus, 1 is the "zero vector" of this space!
  5. Inverse Element ($x'$): We require $x \oplus x' = e = 1 \implies x \cdot x' = 1 \implies \mathbf{x' = rac{1}{x}}$.
    💡 Why is the inverse 1/x?
    Since $e = 1$, we need $x \cdot x' = 1$. Since $x > 0$, $ rac{1}{x}$ is always a positive real number ($ rac{1}{x} \in V$).
Part II: Verification of Scalar Multiplication Axioms
  1. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: For $k \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x \in V$ ($x > 0$), $k \odot x = x^k > 0 \in V$. (Any positive real raised to any real power is strictly positive).
  2. Distributive over Vector Addition: $$k \odot (x \oplus y) = (xy)^k = x^k \cdot y^k = (k \odot x) \oplus (k \odot y)$$
  3. Distributive over Scalar Addition: $$(k + m) \odot x = x^{k+m} = x^k \cdot x^m = (k \odot x) \oplus (m \odot x)$$
    💡 Why this step works?
    By standard laws of exponents: $x^{a+b} = x^a \cdot x^b$. In our space, multiplication between elements is the vector addition operation $\oplus$, so $x^k \cdot x^m = (k \odot x) \oplus (m \odot x)$.
  4. Scalar Associativity: $(km) \odot x = x^{km} = (x^m)^k = k \odot (m \odot x)$.
  5. Unit Scalar Identity: $1 \odot x = x^1 = x$.
✅ All 10 axioms hold. Therefore, $V = \mathbb{R}^+$ IS a Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$.
Q2 • 16 Marks
Determine whether the set of all $2 imes 2$ matrices of the form $egin{bmatrix} a & a+b \ a+b & b \end{bmatrix}$ ($a, b \in \mathbb{R}$) is a vector space under standard matrix addition and scalar multiplication.
Step 1: Check Closure Under Addition

Let $A, B \in V$ with real parameters $(a_1, b_1)$ and $(a_2, b_2)$:

$$A = egin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_1+b_1 \ a_1+b_1 & b_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = egin{bmatrix} a_2 & a_2+b_2 \ a_2+b_2 & b_2 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$A + B = egin{bmatrix} a_1+a_2 & (a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2) \ (a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2) & b_1+b_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Let $a_3 = a_1 + a_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b_3 = b_1 + b_2 \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $A + B = egin{bmatrix} a_3 & a_3+b_3 \ a_3+b_3 & b_3 \end{bmatrix} \in V$.

💡 Why this step?
For a matrix set to be closed, the sum of two matrices must retain the exact same algebraic pattern: (top-left) + (bottom-right) must equal both off-diagonal entries. Here $a_3 + b_3$ matches both off-diagonals, so it stays in $V$.
Step 2: Check Zero Matrix, Inverses, and Scalar Multiplication
  • Zero Matrix: Setting $a = 0, b = 0 \implies egin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in V$.
  • Additive Inverse: For $A$, $-A = egin{bmatrix} -a & -a-b \ -a-b & -b \end{bmatrix} \in V$ (parameters $-a, -b \in \mathbb{R}$).
  • Scalar Multiplication: For any scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$: $$c A = egin{bmatrix} ca & c(a+b) \ c(a+b) & cb \end{bmatrix} = egin{bmatrix} (ca) & (ca)+(cb) \ (ca)+(cb) & (cb) \end{bmatrix} \in V$$
💡 Why does this prove it is a vector space?
Because $V$ is a subset of the known vector space $M_{2 imes 2}(\mathbb{R})$ and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication containing $0$, $V$ is a subspace of $M_{2 imes 2}(\mathbb{R})$. Every subspace is automatically a vector space in its own right!
✅ $V$ satisfies all axioms and IS a Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$.

  2. $2c_1 - 5c_2 = -5 \implies 2(2) - 5c_2 = -5 \implies -5c_2 = -9 \implies c_2 = rac{9}{5}$
  3. Check in equation 3: $-7c_2 = 7 \implies -7\left( rac{9}{5} ight) = - rac{63}{5} eq 7$ (Contradiction!)

A vector is a linear combination if and only if the system of equations has a consistent solution. Here, equation 3 demands $c_2 = -1$, but equation 2 demands $c_2 = 1.8$. Since no unique scalars exist, the system is inconsistent.
❌ $v=(2,-5,7)$ CANNOT be expressed as a linear combination of $S$.
Part (ii) Solution: Linear Independence Test (Row Reduction)

Set $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + c_3 v_3 = 0$. Form the matrix with vectors as rows and reduce to Echelon form:

$$A = egin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \ 2 & 1 & -1 & 2 \ 3 & -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\sim egin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\sim egin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
💡 Why does a zero row mean Linearly Dependent?
The complete zero row in $R_3$ reveals that Row 3 is a direct combination of Row 1 and Row 2 ($v_3 = v_1 + v_2$). The rank of the matrix is $ ho(A) = 2$, which is strictly less than the number of vectors (3). Thus non-zero scalar solutions exist!

Q5(i) • 8 Marks
State and prove the necessary and sufficient conditions for a non-empty subset $W$ of a vector space $V(F)$ to be a subspace.
Statement

A non-empty subset $W$ of a vector space $V(F)$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if:

$$lpha u + eta v \in W \quad ext{for all } u, v \in W ext{ and all scalars } lpha, eta \in F$$
Proof: Necessary Condition ($\implies$)

Assume $W$ is a subspace of $V$.

By definition of a subspace, $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition:

  • For $lpha \in F$ and $u \in W \implies lpha u \in W$.
  • For $eta \in F$ and $v \in W \implies eta v \in W$.

Since $W$ is closed under addition, $(lpha u) + (eta v) \in W$. Hence the condition is necessary.

Proof: Sufficient Condition ($\impliedby$)

Assume $lpha u + eta v \in W$ for all $u, v \in W$ and $lpha, eta \in F$. We must prove $W$ satisfies all subspace criteria:

  1. Zero Vector ($0 \in W$): Choose $lpha = 0, eta = 0$. Then: $$0 \cdot u + 0 \cdot v = 0 + 0 = 0 \in W$$
  2. Closure under Addition: Choose $lpha = 1, eta = 1$. Then: $$1 \cdot u + 1 \cdot v = u + v \in W$$
  3. Additive Inverse: Choose $lpha = -1, eta = 0$. Then: $$(-1)u + 0 \cdot v = -u \in W$$
  4. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: Choose $lpha = c \in F, eta = 0$. Then: $$c \cdot u + 0 \cdot v = c \cdot u \in W$$
  5. Remaining axioms: Associativity, commutativity, and distributive laws hold automatically because $W \subseteq V$ inherits them from $V$.
💡 Why this step?
Proving sufficiency requires showing that the single compact condition $lpha u + eta v \in W$ guarantees all the individual group axioms (Identity, Inverse, Closures) simply by selecting specific values for $lpha$ and $eta$.
Hence proved: $W$ is a subspace of $V$.
Q6(i) • 8 Marks
If $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of a vector space $V$, prove that $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $W_1 \subseteq W_2$ or $W_2 \subseteq W_1$.
Part 1: Sufficient Condition ($\impliedby$)

Assume $W_1 \subseteq W_2$ or $W_2 \subseteq W_1$.

  • If $W_1 \subseteq W_2 \implies W_1 \cup W_2 = W_2$. Since $W_2$ is a subspace, $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace.
  • If $W_2 \subseteq W_1 \implies W_1 \cup W_2 = W_1$. Since $W_1$ is a subspace, $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace.
Part 2: Necessary Condition by Contradiction ($\implies$)

Assume $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$.

Suppose on the contrary that $W_1 ot\subseteq W_2$ and $W_2 ot\subseteq W_1$.

  1. Since $W_1 ot\subseteq W_2$, there exists a vector $x \in W_1$ such that $x otin W_2$.
  2. Since $W_2 ot\subseteq W_1$, there exists a vector $y \in W_2$ such that $y otin W_1$.
  3. Clearly, $x, y \in W_1 \cup W_2$. Since $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace, it must be closed under addition: $$x + y \in W_1 \cup W_2 \implies x + y \in W_1 \quad ext{or} \quad x + y \in W_2$$
  4. Case 1: If $x + y \in W_1$: $$y = (x + y) - x \in W_1 \quad ( ext{since both } x+y, x \in W_1)$$ This contradicts our choice that $y otin W_1$!
  5. Case 2: If $x + y \in W_2$: $$x = (x + y) - y \in W_2 \quad ( ext{since both } x+y, y \in W_2)$$ This contradicts our choice that $x otin W_2$!
💡 Why Proof by Contradiction?
The geometric intuition is that the union of two lines through the origin (like the x-axis and y-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$) is not a subspace because adding a vector on the x-axis $(1,0)$ and a vector on the y-axis $(0,1)$ produces $(1,1)$, which leaves the union. Contradiction shows that the union can only be closed if one subspace completely contains the other.
Hence proved: $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace iff $W_1 \subseteq W_2$ or $W_2 \subseteq W_1$.
Q7(i) • 8 Marks
For which value of $k$ will the vector $v = (1, -2, k)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ be a linear combination of $u = (3, 0, -2)$ and $w = (2, -1, -5)$?
Step-by-Step Solution

Set up the linear combination equation: $v = c_1 u + c_2 w$

$$(1, -2, k) = c_1(3, 0, -2) + c_2(2, -1, -5) = (3c_1 + 2c_2, \; -c_2, \; -2c_1 - 5c_2)$$

Equating components gives 3 equations:

  1. $3c_1 + 2c_2 = 1$
  2. $-c_2 = -2 \implies \mathbf{c_2 = 2}$
  3. $-2c_1 - 5c_2 = k$

Substitute $c_2 = 2$ into equation (1):

$$3c_1 + 2(2) = 1 \implies 3c_1 + 4 = 1 \implies 3c_1 = -3 \implies \mathbf{c_1 = -1}$$

Now substitute $c_1 = -1$ and $c_2 = 2$ into equation (3) to find $k$:

$$k = -2(-1) - 5(2) = 2 - 10 = \mathbf{-8}$$
💡 Why this step?
The first two equations contain only two unknowns ($c_1, c_2$), allowing us to solve them directly. The third equation determines the exact third coordinate $k$ needed to make the vector lie in the 2D plane spanned by $u$ and $w$.
Answer: $k = -8$
Q8(ii) • 8 Marks
Let $S = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ where $v_1=(2,1,0), v_2=(-3,-3,1), v_3=(-2,1,-1)$. Show that $S$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Step-by-Step Solution

We know that the dimension of the vector space $\mathbb{R}^3$ is $\dim(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3$.

💡 Why the Dimension Theorem Shortcut saves 10 minutes in the exam?
By the Basis Theorem: In an $n$-dimensional space, any set of exactly $n$ vectors that is Linearly Independent automatically spans the space! Therefore, we do NOT need to prove spanning separately—we only need to show $|A| eq 0$!

Construct the $3 imes 3$ matrix $A$ with vectors as rows:

$$|A| = egin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \ -3 & -3 & 1 \ -2 & 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix}$$ $$egin{aligned} |A| &= 2[(-3)(-1) - (1)(1)] - 1[(-3)(-1) - (1)(-2)] + 0 \ &= 2(3 - 1) - 1(3 + 2) \ &= 2(2) - 1(5) = 4 - 5 = \mathbf{-1 eq 0} \end{aligned}$$

Since $|A| = -1 eq 0$:

  1. The vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ are Linearly Independent.
  2. Since there are 3 linearly independent vectors in a 3-dimensional space ($\dim(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3$), $S$ also spans $\mathbb{R}^3$.
✅ $S = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ is a Basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
