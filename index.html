QB • Q1
Define Vector Space.

A non-empty set $V$ is called a Vector Space over a field $F$, denoted $V(F)$, under vector addition ($+$) and scalar multiplication ($\cdot$), if for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$, the following 10 axioms hold:

A1. Closure under Addition: $u + v \in V$
A2. Associative Law: $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$
A3. Additive Identity: $\exists\, 0 \in V$ such that $u + 0 = 0 + u = u$
A4. Additive Inverse: $\exists\, -u \in V$ such that $u + (-u) = 0$
A5. Commutative Law: $u + v = v + u$
M1. Closure under Scalar Mult: $\alpha \cdot u \in V$
M2. Distributive over Vector Addition: $\alpha(u + v) = \alpha u + \alpha v$
M3. Distributive over Scalar Addition: $(\alpha + \beta)u = \alpha u + \beta u$
M4. Scalar Associative Law: $(\alpha \beta)u = \alpha(\beta u)$
M5. Unit Scalar: $1 \cdot u = u$ (where $1$ is unity of $F$).
A vector space bridges geometry and algebra. The first 5 axioms $(A_1-A_5)$ make $(V,+)$ an Abelian group (so you can add vectors and cancel them). The next 5 axioms $(M_1-M_5)$ guarantee scaling stretches or shrinks vectors predictably without escaping the space.
QB • Q2
State and prove the cancellation law for vector addition.

Statement: In any vector space $V(F)$, if $x, y, z \in V$ such that $x + z = y + z$, then $x = y$.

$$\begin{aligned} x &= x + 0 = x + (z + (-z)) && (\text{Identity \& Inverse}) \\ &= (x + z) + (-z) && (\text{Associative Law}) \\ &= (y + z) + (-z) && (\text{Given: } x + z = y + z) \\ &= y + (z + (-z)) = y + 0 = y \end{aligned}$$
Hence proved: $x = y$
In abstract vector spaces, "subtraction" does not exist as a primitive operation. We can only add the additive inverse $(-z)$ guaranteed by Axiom A4 and use associativity (Axiom A2) to group $(z + (-z))$ into zero!

If $V = \mathbb{R}^3$, verify whether $W = \{(a_1, a_2, a_3) \mid 2a_1 - 7a_2 + a_3 = 0\}$ is a subspace.

Step 1 (Zero Vector Check): Put $(0,0,0)$: $2(0) - 7(0) + (0) = 0 \implies (0,0,0) \in W$.

Step 2 (Linear Combination Check): Let $u, v \in W$. For any scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$\begin{aligned} &2(\alpha a_1 + \beta b_1) - 7(\alpha a_2 + \beta b_2) + (\alpha a_3 + \beta b_3) \\ &= \alpha(2a_1 - 7a_2 + a_3) + \beta(2b_1 - 7b_2 + b_3) \\ &= \alpha(0) + \beta(0) = 0 \end{aligned}$$
Conclusion: $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$
QB • Q4
Define Subspace.

Let $V$ be a vector space over field $F$. A non-empty subset $W \subseteq V$ is called a subspace of $V$ if $W$ is itself a vector space over $F$ under the operations of addition and scalar multiplication defined on $V$.

Subspace Criterion: $W$ is a subspace iff:
1. $0 \in W$
2. $\alpha u + \beta v \in W$ for all $u, v \in W$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$.
QB • Q5
Define Linear Combination.

Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ and $S = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\} \subseteq V$. A vector $v \in V$ is said to be a linear combination of $S$ if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n \in F$ such that:

$$v = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_n v_n = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i v_i$$

QB • Q7
Define Basis.

A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is called a basis of $V$ if:

1. $S$ is Linearly Independent.
2. $S$ spans $V$ ($\operatorname{Span}(S) = V$).

Find dimension of $W = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \mid x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\}$.

Constraint gives $x_1 = -x_2 - x_3$. Any vector in $W$ is:

$$(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (-x_2 - x_3, x_2, x_3) = x_2(-1, 1, 0) + x_3(-1, 0, 1)$$

Basis is $B = \{(-1, 1, 0), (-1, 0, 1)\}$ (2 vectors).

Conclusion: $\dim(W) = 2$ (Formula: $3 - 1 = 2$)

QB • Q10
If $V = A \oplus B$, show that $\dim V = \dim A + \dim B$.

By dimension formula: $\dim(A + B) = \dim A + \dim B - \dim(A \cap B)$.

Since $V = A \oplus B$ (Direct Sum):
1. $A + B = V \implies \dim(A + B) = \dim V$
2. $A \cap B = \{0\} \implies \dim(A \cap B) = 0$ $$\dim V = \dim A + \dim B - 0 \implies \mathbf{\dim V = \dim A + \dim B}$$
Hence proved: $\dim V = \dim A + \dim B$

Verify whether $W = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x + 2y - 3z = 1\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Put $(x, y, z) = (0, 0, 0)$:

$$0 + 2(0) - 3(0) = 0 \neq 1 \implies (0,0,0) \notin W$$

Extra • Q15
Is $\mathbb{R}$ a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$? Justify.

Answer: No, $\mathbb{R}$ is not a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$.

Reason: Scalar multiplication closure fails.
Take scalar $i \in \mathbb{C}$ and vector $1 \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $i \cdot 1 = i \notin \mathbb{R}$.
Extra • Q16
Prove that the intersection of two subspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$ of $V$ is also a subspace of $V$.

Let $u, v \in W_1 \cap W_2$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$.

1. Since $u, v \in W_1$ and $W_1$ is a subspace $\implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_1$.
2. Since $u, v \in W_2$ and $W_2$ is a subspace $\implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_2$.
Therefore, $\alpha u + \beta v \in W_1 \cap W_2$.
Hence $W_1 \cap W_2$ is a subspace of $V$.

Part B • Q1 (16 Marks)
Let $V$ be the set of all positive real numbers ($\mathbb{R}^+$). Define vector addition as $x \oplus y = xy$ and scalar multiplication as $k \odot x = x^k$ ($k \in \mathbb{R}$). Determine whether or not $V$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$.
Step 1: Check Abelian Group Under Addition $(V, \oplus)$

1. Closure: For $x > 0, y > 0 \implies x \oplus y = xy > 0 \in V$.

2. Commutativity: $x \oplus y = xy = yx = y \oplus x$.

3. Associativity: $(x \oplus y) \oplus z = (xy)z = x(yz) = x \oplus (y \oplus z)$.

4. Additive Identity ($e$): $x \oplus e = x \implies x \cdot e = x \implies \mathbf{e = 1 \in V}$.

5. Additive Inverse ($x'$): $x \oplus x' = e = 1 \implies x \cdot x' = 1 \implies \mathbf{x' = \frac{1}{x} \in V}$ (since $x > 0 \implies 1/x > 0$).

Step 2: Check Scalar Multiplication Axioms

6. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: For $k \in \mathbb{R}, x > 0 \implies k \odot x = x^k > 0 \in V$.

7. Distributive over Vector Addition: $k \odot (x \oplus y) = (xy)^k = x^k y^k = (k \odot x) \oplus (k \odot y)$.

8. Distributive over Scalar Addition: $(k + m) \odot x = x^{k+m} = x^k \cdot x^m = (k \odot x) \oplus (m \odot x)$.

9. Scalar Associative Law: $(km) \odot x = x^{km} = (x^m)^k = k \odot (m \odot x)$.

10. Unit Scalar Identity: $1 \odot x = x^1 = x$.

✅ All 10 axioms hold! $V = \mathbb{R}^+$ IS a Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$.
In this problem, the operation symbol $\oplus$ means real multiplication ($xy$). The identity for multiplication is $1$ (since $x \times 1 = x$). Thus, $1$ acts as the abstract "zero vector" of this space!
Part B • Q2 (16 Marks)
Determine whether the set of all $2 \times 2$ matrices of the form $\begin{bmatrix} a & a+b \\ a+b & b \end{bmatrix}$ ($a, b \in \mathbb{R}$) is a vector space under standard matrix operations.
Step 1: Check Closure Under Addition

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_1+b_1 \\ a_1+b_1 & b_1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} a_2 & a_2+b_2 \\ a_2+b_2 & b_2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$A + B = \begin{bmatrix} a_1+a_2 & (a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2) \\ (a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2) & b_1+b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_3 & a_3+b_3 \\ a_3+b_3 & b_3 \end{bmatrix} \in V$$

Since $a_3 = a_1 + a_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b_3 = b_1 + b_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, $V$ is closed under addition.

Step 2: Check Zero Matrix, Inverses, and Scalar Multiplication

• Zero matrix: Setting $a = 0, b = 0 \implies \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in V$.

• Additive inverse: $-A = \begin{bmatrix} -a & -(a+b) \\ -(a+b) & -b \end{bmatrix} \in V$.

• Scalar multiplication: $c A = \begin{bmatrix} ca & c(a+b) \\ c(a+b) & cb \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ca & ca+cb \\ ca+cb & cb \end{bmatrix} \in V$.

✅ $V$ is a subspace of $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ and therefore IS a Vector Space.
Part B • Q3 (16 Marks)
Consider $V = P_2(\mathbb{R}) = \{a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 \mid a_0, a_1, a_2 \in \mathbb{R}\}$, the set of all polynomials of degree $\le 2$. Prove that $V$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ under usual addition and multiplication.
Proof Outline (All 10 Axioms for Polynomials)

Let $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$ and $q(x) = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2$:

$$p(x) + q(x) = (a_0 + b_0) + (a_1 + b_1)x + (a_2 + b_2)x^2 \in P_2(\mathbb{R})$$ $$c \cdot p(x) = (ca_0) + (ca_1)x + (ca_2)x^2 \in P_2(\mathbb{R})$$

• Zero Polynomial: $0(x) = 0 + 0x + 0x^2 \in P_2(\mathbb{R})$.

• Inverse: $-p(x) = -a_0 - a_1 x - a_2 x^2 \in P_2(\mathbb{R})$.

• Associativity, commutativity, and distributive laws follow directly from real arithmetic on polynomial coefficients.

Hence proved: $P_2(\mathbb{R})$ is a vector space of dimension 3.
Part B • Q4 (16 Marks)
(i) Let $V = \mathbb{R}^3, S = \{(1,2,0), (0,-5,-7)\}$ and $v = (2,-5,7)$. Verify whether $v$ is a linear combination of $S$.
(ii) Let $S = \{(1,-2,3,-1), (2,1,-1,2), (3,-1,2,1)\}$ in $V_4(\mathbb{R})$. Check whether $S$ is linearly independent.
Part (i) Solution: Linear Combination Test

Set $(2, -5, 7) = c_1(1, 2, 0) + c_2(0, -5, -7) = (c_1, \; 2c_1 - 5c_2, \; -7c_2)$:

1. $c_1 = 2$
2. $2(2) - 5c_2 = -5 \implies 4 - 5c_2 = -5 \implies -5c_2 = -9 \implies c_2 = \frac{9}{5}$
3. Check 3rd equation: $-7c_2 = -7\left(\frac{9}{5}\right) = -\frac{63}{5} \neq 7$ (Contradiction!)
Result: $v = (2,-5,7)$ CANNOT be expressed as a linear combination of $S$.
Part (ii) Solution: Row Echelon Form Reduction

Set $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + c_3 v_3 = 0$. Form matrix with vectors as rows:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1,\; R_3 - 3R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -7 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since Row 3 is completely zero, $\operatorname{Rank}(A) = 2 < 3$ (number of vectors).

Result: The set $S$ is Linearly Dependent (LD).
Part B • Q5 (16 Marks)
(i) State and prove necessary and sufficient conditions for a subspace.
(ii) Verify whether $x^3 - 8x^2 + 4x$ can be expressed as a linear combination of $x^3 - 2x^2 + 3x - 1$ and $x^3 - 2x + 3$ in $P_3(\mathbb{R})$.
Part (i): Subspace Criterion Theorem

Statement: $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace iff $\mathbf{\alpha u + \beta v \in W}$ for all $u, v \in W, \alpha, \beta \in F$.

Proof:
• Necessary: Since $W$ is a subspace, it is closed under scalar mult and addition $\implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W$.
• Sufficient: Given $\alpha u + \beta v \in W$:
  $\alpha = 0, \beta = 0 \implies 0 \in W$ (Identity).
  $\alpha = 1, \beta = 1 \implies u + v \in W$ (Closure under addition).
  $\alpha = -1, \beta = 0 \implies -u \in W$ (Inverse).
  $\alpha = c, \beta = 0 \implies cu \in W$ (Closure under scalar mult).
Hence proved: $W$ is a subspace.
Part (ii): Polynomial Linear Combination Test

Set $x^3 - 8x^2 + 4x = c_1(x^3 - 2x^2 + 3x - 1) + c_2(x^3 - 2x + 3)$:

Equate coefficients:
• $x^3$: $c_1 + c_2 = 1$
• $x^2$: $-2c_1 = -8 \implies \mathbf{c_1 = 4}$
From $c_1 = 4 \implies 4 + c_2 = 1 \implies \mathbf{c_2 = -3}$
• Check $x^1$ coefficient: $3c_1 - 2c_2 = 3(4) - 2(-3) = 12 + 6 = 18 \neq 4$ (Contradiction!)
Result: The polynomial CANNOT be expressed as a linear combination of the other two.
Part B • Q6 (16 Marks)
(i) If $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of $V$, prove that $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $W_1 \subseteq W_2$ or $W_2 \subseteq W_1$.
(ii) Verify whether any set of 5 matrices in $M_{2 \times 2}$ is linearly dependent.
Part (i): Proof by Contradiction
• If $W_1 \subseteq W_2 \implies W_1 \cup W_2 = W_2$ (already a subspace).
• Conversely, assume $W_1 \cup W_2$ is a subspace, but $W_1 \not\subseteq W_2$ and $W_2 \not\subseteq W_1$.
Then $\exists\, x \in W_1 \setminus W_2$ and $y \in W_2 \setminus W_1$.
By closure, $x + y \in W_1 \cup W_2$:
  If $x + y \in W_1 \implies y = (x + y) - x \in W_1$ (Contradiction!).
  If $x + y \in W_2 \implies x = (x + y) - y \in W_2$ (Contradiction!).
Therefore, either $W_1 \subseteq W_2$ or $W_2 \subseteq W_1$.
Part (ii): Dimension Bound in $M_{2 \times 2}$

The standard basis of $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ has 4 matrices $\implies \dim(M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})) = 4$.

By the Fundamental Theorem: Any set of $m > n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space is always Linearly Dependent. Since $5 > 4$, any 5 matrices are strictly Linearly Dependent!

Part B • Q8 (16 Marks)
(i) Find dimension of $W = \{(a,b,c) \mid 2a + 3b = c, \; 7c + 2b = a\}$.
(ii) Let $S = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ where $v_1=(2,1,0), v_2=(-3,-3,1), v_3=(-2,1,-1)$. Show that $S$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Part (i): Solve Homogeneous System for $W$
Equations: (1) $2a + 3b - c = 0$, and (2) $a - 2b - 7c = 0 \implies a = 2b + 7c$.
Substitute into (1): $2(2b + 7c) + 3b - c = 0 \implies 7b + 13c = 0 \implies b = -\frac{13}{7}c$.
Then $a = 2\left(-\frac{13}{7}c\right) + 7c = \frac{23}{7}c$.
Vector in $W$: $(a,b,c) = c\left(\frac{23}{7}, -\frac{13}{7}, 1\right)$.
Part (i) Answer: Basis is $\{(23, -13, 7)\}$ and $\dim(W) = 1$.
Part (ii): Basis Verification of $\mathbb{R}^3$

Evaluate determinant of the 3 vectors:

$$|A| = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & -3 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 2(3 - 1) - 1(3 + 2) + 0 = 4 - 5 = \mathbf{-1 \neq 0}$$
Since $|A| = -1 \neq 0$, $S$ is Linearly Independent and spans $\mathbb{R}^3$. Thus $S$ is a Basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Part B • Q9 (16 Marks)
(i) Show that $\{(1,1,0), (0,1,1), (1,0,1)\}$ forms a basis for $V_3(\mathbb{R})$.
(ii) Determine the basis and dimension of the solution space of the system: $x + y - z = 0$; $-2x - y + 2z = 0$; $-x + z = 0$.
Part (i): Determinant Check
$$|A| = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1(1 - 0) - 1(0 - 1) + 0 = 1 + 1 = \mathbf{2 \neq 0}$$
Since $|A| = 2 \neq 0$, the set is linearly independent and forms a Basis for $V_3(\mathbb{R})$.
Part (ii): Null Space / Solution Space Calculation

Perform row operations on the coefficient matrix:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -2 & -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 + 2R_1,\; R_3 + R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ • Equation 2 gives: $\mathbf{y = 0}$.

Let $z = t$ (free variable). Solution vector: $(x, y, z) = (t, 0, t) = t(1, 0, 1)$.

Part B • Q10 (16 Marks)
Show that the vectors $\{(2,-3,1), (0,1,2), (1,1,2)\}$ form a basis for $V_3(\mathbb{R})$.
Step-by-Step Proof

We know $\dim(V_3(\mathbb{R})) = 3$. Any set of 3 linearly independent vectors forms a basis.

$$|A| = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2(2 - 2) - (-3)(0 - 2) + 1(0 - 1) = 0 - 6 - 1 = \mathbf{-7 \neq 0}$$

1. Since $|A| = -7 \neq 0$, the set is Linearly Independent.

2. Since 3 linearly independent vectors exist in a 3-dimensional space, they also span $V_3(\mathbb{R})$.

✅ The vectors form a complete Basis for $V_3(\mathbb{R})$.
